Incompressible Navier–Stokes solver

One solver advances face velocity and cell pressure on a static or dynamically adapted uniform/mixed-level tree. Supplying an interface selects two-phase coefficients and compatible geometric transport. A single FaceVelocity selects the one-velocity model; a (liquid_velocity, gas_velocity) tuple selects the two-velocity model. The outer time-step sequence stays the same.

Common step

Each step executes transport, midpoint body forcing, implicit diffusion, optional phase forces, and one pressure projection. Dispatch changes the kernel inside a stage rather than selecting a second time integrator.

For single-phase flow, the transport and user-force state is

\[u^c = u^n - \Delta t\,C_{LW}(u^n) + \Delta t\,f^{n+1/2}.\]

The bulk scheme defaults to LaxWendroff() and the scheme-aware AMR-interface default selects MonotonizedCentral() on non-regular coarse–fine connections. Global Upwind retains Upwind at the interface, and another limited bulk scheme is used unchanged. Interface-only Upwind is rejected because its unmatched first-order closure has poor mixed-level accuracy. amr_interface_scheme can still be overridden with any compatible non-Upwind scheme.

For two-phase flow, directionally split Weymouth–Yue interface advection first computes the liquid and gas volumes crossing every centred face. The momentum stage interpolates those same volume fluxes to staggered control volumes and advects the two intrinsic phase velocities with CTU upwinding near the interface. Only intrinsic velocity is stored. Compatible phase scalars follow the same construction, so a scalar equal to one in either phase is recovered exactly. Intrinsic recovery divides only above sqrt(eps(T)) phase fraction.

The generic user force f is sampled at the time midpoint and added to the transported state before diffusion in both models. Construct a flow with force=(x, y, time, axis) -> ...; the callback is stored as flow.force and defaults to nothing. Both step!(flow, dt) and simulate!(flow, ...) use this stored force unless it is overridden for the call. Single-phase viscosity is then centred by Crank–Nicolson/implicit midpoint:

\[\left(I-\frac{\Delta t\nu}{2}L\right)u^{**} = u^c + \frac{\Delta t\nu}{2}Lu^n + \Delta t\nu b^{n+1/2}_{wall}.\]

The affine wall term contains both half-step contributions. The shifted system is solved independently on the x- and y-dual grids by StaggeredEllipticSolver. It selects external Krylov.jl CG for a symmetric operator and BiCGSTAB otherwise, with the package's geometric V-cycle preconditioner. The V-cycle objects contain no Krylov logic. The unchanged operator, preconditioner, and workspaces are reused for a fixed dt, and LU appears only in the two geometric bottom solves.

For two phases, the viscous term is the coupled stress divergence div(2*mu*D(u)). At cell-centred normal stresses and vertex-centred shear stresses, the dynamic viscosity is a mixture of the phase viscosities with the liquid fraction c of the open part of a cell: harmonic (series) by default,

\[\bar\mu=\left(\frac{c}{\mu^\ell}+\frac{1-c}{\mu^g}\right)^{-1},\]

which transmits the tangential traction across an aligned interface. The thesis' geometric mixture \bar\mu=(\mu^\ell)^c(\mu^g)^{1-c} remains available through NavierStokesOptions(viscosity_averaging = GeometricViscosityAveraging()) (HarmonicViscosityAveraging() is the default). A stress row that spans several cell quarters averages with the same rule: the weighted harmonic mean by default, and the geometric mean, evaluated with the interpolated fraction, for the geometric option. Density appears in the staggered inertia matrix, not in this dynamic viscosity. The midpoint system is

\[\left(M_\rho+\frac{\Delta t}{2}K_\mu\right)u^{**} =M_\rho u^*-\frac{\Delta t}{2}K_\mu u^*,\]

where K_mu is the positive-semidefinite integrated stress stiffness. Here u* is the state after compatible momentum transport and any generic body force applied before diffusion, matching the split stress mu*symgrad(u* + u**). This staggered variable-density/variable-viscosity construction and the two-velocity diffusion model are documented by Remmerswaal (2023); related finite-volume Stokes solver structure is described by Cai et al. (2014).

Balanced CSF or CGFM surface tension and gravity are added after diffusion using a weighted-gradient discretization compatible with the pressure operator. Denote the state after these forces by $u^H$ and let $G$ include inverse density and the appropriate geometric coefficients. The one-velocity and CMDGFM projections form a pressure-predicted state and solve for the pressure correction $\phi$:

\[u^* = u^H - \Delta t\,G p^n,\qquad -DG\phi = -\frac{Du^*-D^n u^n}{\Delta t} -\frac{D^n u^n}{\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}}, \qquad u^{n+1}=u^*-\Delta t\,G\phi,\qquad p^{n+1}=p^n+\phi.\]

For the one-velocity two-phase model, G includes the face coefficient 1/<rho>, where <rho>=rho_l*c_f+rho_g*(1-c_f). The same finite-volume D and its dual-derived G are used in the Poisson matrix, old-pressure predictor, and velocity correction.

For these incremental projections, $D^n u^n$ is the inherited divergence measured on the old state and old apertures. In simulate!, the reference step $\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the maximum of the unshortened proposal, the current step, and the previous actual step when available. After a regrid, the new transfer defect instead uses the proposal associated with that transfer (and with the two-velocity regrid projection). A direct step!(flow, dt) uses the maximum of dt and any retained defect reference; the field-level step! defaults its reference to the supplied step. An ordinary step with equal actual and reference steps recovers the usual full projection. Otherwise the solve removes the fraction $\Delta t/\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}$ of the inherited defect rather than dividing all of it by the short step. Up to solver residual and boundary enforcement, the remaining defect is $(1-\Delta t/\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}})D^n u^n$. This uses the same solve, not an additional projection.

An input pressure supplied to FlowSimulation is the initial physical pressure; if omitted, it is zero. A fresh hydrostatic calculation therefore starts with $p^0=0$; its first projection builds the pressure. A nonzero initial pressure enters the first incremental predictor. Resumed calculations use the retained physical pressure, including after transfer to an adapted mesh. The standalone project!(flow) can prepare the initial velocity constraint; it uses scratch pressure for that geometric impulse and leaves flow.pressure unchanged. The lower-level one-velocity and CMDGFM project! methods instead add their solved increments to the supplied pressure and expect an input velocity already predicted with that pressure. Repeatedly projecting an unpredicted velocity with a reused pressure field therefore accumulates impulses; pass fresh zero pressure for an absolute velocity projection.

Two-velocity projection

TwoVelocityModel selects its pressure projection through the pressure_projection field; CMDGFMProjection() is the only supported choice and the default. Its interface transport uses the frozen liquid/gas subfaces described in Two-velocity advection.

CMDGFMProjection() solves the nonsymmetric two-block system

\[\begin{bmatrix}L_{pp}&L_{p\xi}\\L_{\xi p}&L_{\xi\xi}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\phi\\\xi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b_p\\b_\xi\end{bmatrix}.\]

It uses the same conservative D, AMR Riesz gradient, pressure block, staggered phase fractions, PLIC apertures, and cut-cell geometry. The local jump interpolant uses transverse incident faces and a bounded coefficient. Its jump rows enforce normal-velocity continuity also on shortened steps; only the mixture-divergence target retains an inherited defect. Writing R=|n_t/n_f|, gamma_lg=gamma^l_(h^g), and gamma_gl=gamma^g_(h^l), the implemented choice is

\[\beta_f=\begin{cases} 1,&R\leq1,\\ \dfrac{1-\gamma_{lg}-\gamma_{gl}} {R-\gamma_{lg}-\gamma_{gl}},&R>1. \end{cases}\]

Both inference faces are neighboring same-axis faces. This leaves the jump block diagonal equal to one and makes every row weakly diagonally dominant. BiCGSTAB uses a lower triangular block preconditioner. The pressure action is one application of the existing rediscretized tree-geometric GMG hierarchy, mapped between the full compatible pressure space and CMDGFM's gauge-reduced unknowns. The jump action is a Crout ILU of the active jump block. A jump is active only when both staggered phase fractions exceed fraction_floor and its local interpolation stencil is resolved.

On a fixed pressure space the GMG hierarchy is retained and, by default, its coefficients are refreshed when they change. The jump ILU is reused only when the active-face ordering and matrix entries are unchanged. After a mesh update, topology-dependent pressure data is rebuilt while reusable hierarchy, Krylov, and geometric-template storage is retained where compatible. Set refresh_pressure_coefficients=false to retain a lagged hierarchy or reuse_jump_factorization=false to force jump-factor rebuilds. CMDGFMProjection(preconditioner=:ilu) remains available as a whole-system ILU comparison.

Momentum and pressure-work diagnostics use the weak face inner product induced by the same Riesz map. This equals the familiar diagonal dual-volume kinetic inner product on a uniform Cartesian mesh; at a coarse–fine interface it includes the vertex-patch couplings used by the AMR gradient.

Two-velocity diffusion

Viscosity uses an equivalent-one-velocity construction. With the liquid fraction c_f in an open staggered dual volume, the transported intrinsic velocities first form

\[\bar u^* = \frac{\rho^\ell c_f u^{\ell,*} + \rho^g(1-c_f)u^{g,*}} {\rho^\ell c_f + \rho^g(1-c_f)}.\]

The existing one-velocity two-phase midpoint solver advances this field using the same variable-density inertia and the same phase-viscosity mixture: the viscosity_averaging rule of NavierStokesOptions governs the one stress operator that both velocity models share, so it applies to two-velocity flows as well. Both phases then receive the common increment

\[u^{\pi,**}=u^{\pi,*}+(\bar u^{**}-\bar u^*).\]

The phase-weighted momentum therefore matches the one-velocity solve, the intrinsic jump u_g-u_liquid is preserved, and equal phase velocities recover the one-velocity result exactly. On embedded geometry, c_f is weighted over the open part of the staggered dual volume. Only intrinsic velocities are stored; phase-weighted values are local temporaries.

The integration suite also advances the finite-viscosity La = 3000 capillary wave over a quarter period on 8, 16, and 32 cells per wavelength. It compares the complete amplitude history with Prosperetti's linear solution and with the one-velocity solver on the same meshes and time steps:

Dimensionless solver tolerance

step! and simulate! expose one dimensionless tolerance, shared by the pressure and diffusion solves. They do not expose algebraic rtol or dimensional atol values.

For pressure, the implementation follows Basilisk. If r_p is the strong Poisson residual, the requested stopping condition is

\[\Delta t^2\lVert r_p\rVert_\infty \leq \mathtt{tolerance}.\]

This quantity measures the fractional volume defect associated with the linear-solve residual. On a shortened step the intended, deliberately retained inherited divergence above is additional; boundary enforcement can also change the final divergence.

For diffusion, the integrated momentum residual is divided by the local staggered inertia. This gives a velocity defect. Its infinity norm is divided by the corresponding strong right-hand-side velocity scale, so the same dimensionless tolerance applies to both single- and variable-density solves. This normalization depends on the equation data, not on the initial guess. Both criteria use a floating-point roundoff floor from the starting solve state. A current-iterate floor can increase the stop level only after the residual decreases and while that floor is less than 1% of the initial residual.

LinearSolverStats reports the initial and final L2 and infinity norms, the effective infinity-norm target, the residual scale, iterations, the componentwise removed strong pressure-RHS means and their volume-weighted aggregate over unanchored components, Krylov method, and preconditioner. removed_rhs_component_anchors identifies the smallest cell index of each unanchored component in the same order as removed_rhs_component_means. For one-velocity projections, compatible is true exactly when dt^2 * maximum(abs, removed_rhs_component_means) does not exceed the requested projection tolerance (the maximum is zero when there are no components). This compares the mean in the same dimensionless units as the projection residual, using the actual step and the RHS formed with its reference step. Unlike converged, it does not use the roundoff-raised linear solve target: a projected linear solve can converge even if compatible is false. The flag and component values also appear in the simulation summary. An incompatible projection emits a warning once per projection workspace, including across mesh rebuilds and subsequent calls using that workspace. A failed high-level solve includes the diagnostics, requested tolerance, achieved dimensionless residual, iteration cap, timestep, and simulation time.

Boundary conditions

The default wall conditions on non-periodic sides impose no permeation, u dot n = 0. The pressure increment has homogeneous Neumann data there, so projection cannot change the prescribed boundary-normal velocity. For each connected component without an open pressure boundary, the one-velocity increment has volume-weighted zero mean. On a connected unanchored domain the accumulated physical pressure retains its incoming mean; when the domain has several components, the unanchored component means are set to zero. A component with unbalanced prescribed boundary flux retains a divergence contribution even when the linear solve converges: inspect compatible and the componentwise removed RHS means, or use compatibility=:error to reject such data. The two-velocity systems anchor each connected pressure component. The accumulated CMDGFM pressure retains its incoming anchor gauge.

The viscous closure is independent:

  • NoSlip() is the default and uses zero tangential wall velocity;
  • NoSlip(wall_velocity) supports a moving wall such as a cavity lid; and
  • FreeSlip() imposes zero tangential shear.

For open boundaries, VelocityBoundaryConditions(; left, right, bottom, top) selects a condition per side (unspecified sides default to NoSlip()). Inflow(f) prescribes velocity from f(x, y, time, axis). When applied to initialize a boundary, ZeroGradientVelocity() extrapolates the normal velocity from the interior. Outflow() uses area- and aperture-weighted extrapolation, then applies a uniform outward correction over outflow faces to make the total boundary flux zero. Without either kind of open side, prescribed boundary velocities must already have zero net flux. WY interface transport requires impermeable boundaries.

The two-velocity pressure projection retains its supplied phase boundary fluxes instead of applying an open-pressure correction. Its boundary preparation requires each phase's net prescribed flux to balance, unless an Outflow() supplies the balancing correction; an incompatible flux with only ZeroGradientVelocity() open sides raises ArgumentError. Standalone two-velocity projections consume already-prepared boundary values, so callers must also ensure the componentwise mixture flux is compatible.

For one velocity, initial open-face velocities are input data, not automatically extrapolated. In particular, a FaceVelocity filled only on interior faces still has zero boundary values. If an extrapolated initial outlet is wanted, call enforce_velocity_boundary!(velocity, boundary; geometry=geometry) once before the initial projection or simulation (omit geometry without a solid). Do not repeat this after projection: it would overwrite the corrected flux.

The one-velocity pressure increment uses homogeneous Dirichlet data on Outflow() and ZeroGradientVelocity() normal faces and homogeneous Neumann data on fixed-velocity faces. An open face therefore participates in the projection; its corrected normal velocity is retained rather than re-extrapolated afterward. The old physical pressure is applied with the same weighted gradient, including open faces, before solving for the increment. For a connected fluid region, positive open pressure conductance removes the constant pressure nullspace, so the solver does not subtract the right-hand-side mean or impose a mean-zero pressure. Pure Neumann and periodic problems retain both conventions. During a step, the open predictor is the previous projected boundary velocity plus the area- and aperture-weighted extrapolation of the interior velocity change. This retains the projected flux in the zero-step limit instead of introducing a boundary impulse.

The physical pressure used by the incremental predictor is also evaluated with zero pressure at open faces. Initial pressure data that are inconsistent with that boundary value, including a nonzero hydrostatic outlet pressure, can cause an initial spurious outlet impulse. The present gravity and surface-tension force discretizations do not supply balancing open-face forces; a vertical open boundary under gravity can therefore drain rather than preserve a hydrostatic state. Use boundary data and a configuration consistent with this pressure condition.

For a standalone one-velocity projection, the pressure operator must use the same boundary configuration, for example EllipticSolver(PoissonOperator(mesh; velocity_boundary=boundary)), and project!(...; velocity_boundary=boundary) must receive that boundary too. The projection retains the supplied open normal velocity as its predictor; call enforce_velocity_boundary! first when extrapolation is desired.

These increment boundary conditions do not by themselves establish high-order physical pressure boundary values. Especially at no-slip walls, a separate boundary-accuracy study is needed before pressure is used as a validation observable. Brown, Cortez, and Minion discuss this distinction for projection methods.

API

mesh = BlockTreeMesh(block_size=(32, 32), periodic=(false, false))
lid = NoSlip((x, y, time, axis) ->
    axis == 1 && y > 0.5 ? 1.0 : 0.0)

system = NavierStokesSystem(
    mesh; density=1.0, dynamic_viscosity=0.01, boundary=lid,
    scheme=LaxWendroff()) # MonotonizedCentral is selected at AMR interfaces
workspace = NavierStokesWorkspace(system)
velocity = FaceVelocity(mesh, (x, y, time, axis) -> 0.0)
pressure = CellField(mesh)

result = simulate!(
    velocity, pressure, system, 1.0, workspace;
    dt=0.002, tolerance=1e-8, max_cycles=80)

The corresponding two-phase setup supplies a physics object:

interface = InterfaceField(
    mesh, (x, y) -> hypot(x - 0.5, y - 0.5) - 0.15)
physics = TwoPhase(
    interface;
    density=(1000.0, 1.0),
    dynamic_viscosity=(1e-3, 1.8e-5),
    surface_tension=0.072,
    gravity=(0.0, -9.81),
)
flow = FlowSimulation(
    mesh, physics; velocity_model=OneVelocityModel(),
    boundary=FreeSlip(), dt=1e-4)

The two-velocity form supplies two intrinsic velocity initializers and uses the same material-property API:

flow = FlowSimulation(
    mesh, physics;
    velocity_model=TwoVelocityModel(
        pressure_projection=CMDGFMProjection(),
    ),
    velocity=(liquid_velocity, gas_velocity),
    boundary=FreeSlip(),
)

examples/07_interfacial_instability/interfacial_instability.jl uses this API for a combined Kelvin–Helmholtz/Rayleigh–Taylor wave with surface tension. The example is parameterized by the liquid-to-gas density ratio, Weber number, and Bond number:

include("examples/07_interfacial_instability/interfacial_instability.jl")
run = run_example(
    density_ratio=1000.0,
    weber=0.25,
    bond=0.5,
    max_level=1,
    final_time=0.1,
)

It uses the capillary frequency as its time scale. With wavenumber k, the derived coefficients satisfy sigma*k^3/(rho_liquid + rho_gas) = 1. Consequently the dispersion relation for a heavy liquid above the gas has offset sqrt(1 - weber - bond). The dimensional density tuple, phase velocity jump, surface tension, and gravity passed to FlowSimulation are derived internally from those nondimensional inputs.

Pressure, single-phase staggered diffusion, and coupled two-phase stress diffusion default to preconditioned Krylov.jl CG because their operators are symmetric positive (semi-)definite. Automatic selection still falls back to BiCGSTAB for a nonsymmetric operator. Pressure and diffusion use rediscretized tree-geometric hierarchies for both single-phase and two-phase flow. The single-phase diffusion hierarchy keeps separate x/y component operators; the two-phase hierarchy retains the coupled stress operator. Both can be configured independently. Below the root level, each hierarchy agglomerates 2×2 base roots until it reaches coarse_size or max_levels, either root count cannot be halved, or a periodic axis would have fewer than four cells. The last sparse operator is the bottom solve even if it still exceeds coarse_size:

import AlgebraicMultigrid, IncompleteLU, Krylov

system = NavierStokesSystem(
    mesh; density=1.0, dynamic_viscosity=0.01, boundary=lid,
    pressure_krylov=Krylov.CgWorkspace,
    pressure_krylov_options=(;),
    pressure_preconditioner=:amg,
    diffusion_krylov=Krylov.BicgstabWorkspace,
    diffusion_krylov_options=(;),
    diffusion_preconditioner=:ilu,
    diffusion_preconditioner_options=(; drop_tolerance=1e-3))

The two *_krylov_options named tuples forward method-specific settings; for example, a GmresWorkspace factory can use diffusion_krylov_options=(; restart=true, reorthogonalization=true). The dimensionless convergence tolerance and iteration ceiling come from step! or simulate!.

Available specifications and their symmetry restrictions are listed in Geometric multigrid and pressure projection.

step!(flow, dt; force=flow.force, observer=NoObserver(), tolerance=sqrt(eps(T)), max_cycles=nothing) performs one complete step and returns the pressure-projection statistics. It advances flow.time and the step, mesh-update, and unresolved-curvature counters, applies due mesh adaptation, rebuilds topology-dependent state when needed, records step diagnostics, and sends the observer a :step event. It throws if the pressure projection does not converge. Here T is the flow's scalar type, and max_cycles=nothing selects 100 cycles for one phase or 500 for two phases.

For lower-level control, step!(velocity, pressure, system, dt, workspace; time=..., force=...) performs the physical solve but does not manage flow time or counters, post-step adaptation, diagnostics, or observer events. Its returned statistics report projection convergence to the caller.

simulate!(flow, final_time; ...) repeatedly performs full steps to the absolute final time. It uses flow.force unless overridden. If dt is omitted, the solver combines the staggered convective CFL bound with the two-phase capillary and gravity-wave restrictions when those forces are present. The cfl keyword must lie in (0, 1] for one phase and (0, 1/2] for two phases; outside that range simulate! and stable_timestep throw an ArgumentError that names the range, also when a prescribed dt makes cfl unused. Each step is checked against the same bound at the top of that range, so every step the bound returns is accepted and cfl alone sets the margin. The implicit-midpoint diffusion stage imposes no diffusion-based timestep restriction; pass dt explicitly when a particular temporal accuracy resolution is required. Crank–Nicolson is second-order but does not strongly damp unresolved stiff modes, so reduce the time step when a viscous transient oscillates even though the implicit solve converges.

When a proposed step would leave less than 1e-6 of that proposal before the target time, simulate! stretches it to land on the target instead. The unmodified automatic proposal is retained for a subsequent call when the fields, system, time, workspace, and topology still match. A full step!(flow, dt) resets the automatic proposal while retaining its defect reference; a field-level step! or project!(flow) invalidates both. Stability checks allow a relative excess of 1e-6 plus roundoff, including for explicitly requested steps and advection calls, to accommodate this endpoint adjustment.

Pass an observer such as a Recorder with observer=recorder to store copied frames at scheduled keyframes:

fields = VisualizationFieldRegistry()
register_default_fields!(fields, flow)
recorder = Recorder(fields; diagnostics=flow, keyframes=EquispacedTimes(21))
result = simulate!(flow, final_time; observer=recorder)
store = snapshot_store(recorder)

The observer is optional: diagnostics are owned and evaluated by the simulation whether or not a recorder is present. Each full step! emits :step after adaptation and diagnostic recording. simulate! additionally emits :initial, :completed, and, on failure, :failed through observe!; a requested stop is honored at a step boundary (stopped = true). At the initial event, it provides an independent-step procedure used for exact keyframes without changing the main time-step sequence. Exact keyframes require a fixed mesh. See Recording and visualization for scheduling, Studio, and static export.

Dynamic refinement is enabled by passing refinement_indicators and optional additional adaptive_fields to the solver constructor. Velocity and pressure are always transferred. Adaptation happens after a completed physical step, subject to mesh.adaptation.options.update_interval, without an additional physical time step. Two-velocity flows project the transferred velocity without replacing the transferred pressure. Topology-dependent operators and workspaces are rebuilt only when the leaf set changes. See Dynamic adaptive refinement for the API, transfer invariants, cooldown, benchmarks, and exact-keyframe limitation.

Accuracy notes

The complete time step is verified with periodic Taylor–Green flow on uniform and mixed-level meshes. The lid-driven-cavity example provides a familiar viscous reference with moving and stationary no-slip walls. As with other projection methods, velocity and divergence are the primary observables; accurate physical pressure at a no-slip boundary requires a separate boundary treatment and refinement study.
